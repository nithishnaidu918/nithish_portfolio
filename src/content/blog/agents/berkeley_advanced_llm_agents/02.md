# Learning to Self-Improve and Reason with LLMs

## 1. Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) can answer questions, write code, explain concepts, and follow instructions. However, they can still hallucinate, follow irrelevant information, agree with incorrect user statements, and struggle to evaluate their own answers.

This raises an important research question:

**Can an LLM learn to improve its own answers, reasoning, and ability to judge answer quality?**

This lecture explores ways to build models that can:

- Reason more carefully before answering.
- Identify and reduce errors and biases.
- Generate their own training examples.
- Evaluate the quality of their responses.
- Learn from preferences and rewards.
- Improve their reasoning and evaluation capabilities over repeated training cycles.

The long-term goal is to reduce dependence on human supervision while making AI systems more capable and reliable.

However, self-improvement does not mean a model automatically becomes smarter whenever it uses itself. The quality of its generated data, evaluations, rewards, and training process matters.

# Part 1: Understanding Reasoning in LLMs

## 2. System 1 and System 2 reasoning

The lecture uses the distinction between System 1 and System 2 as a way to understand different styles of reasoning.

### System 1: Fast and reactive

System 1 produces responses quickly using learned patterns and associations.

Traditional language-model generation resembles this style: the model predicts the next token based on its input and previously generated tokens.

For example, when asked, "What is the capital of France?", a model can immediately respond, "Paris."

System 1 is useful because it is fast and efficient. However, relying on learned associations can lead to errors when a question is unfamiliar, misleading, or contains irrelevant information.

Possible failures include:

- **Hallucination:** Producing information that is false or unsupported.
- **Sycophancy:** Agreeing with the user's opinion even when it is incorrect.
- **Spurious correlations:** Using an irrelevant association instead of the information that actually matters.
- **Jailbreaking:** Being manipulated by instructions that try to bypass intended safeguards.

### System 2: Deliberate and effortful

System 2 represents a more deliberate problem-solving process.

Instead of immediately accepting the first response, a system can spend additional computation on:

- Planning.
- Breaking a problem into subquestions.
- Searching for information.
- Checking intermediate results.
- Comparing possible answers.
- Verifying conclusions.

For example, rather than immediately answering a complicated mathematical question, a system might divide it into smaller calculations and check whether the results are consistent.

System 2 can be implemented using multiple LLM calls, tools, search procedures, verification steps, or reasoning-oriented training.

**Important:** These are conceptual categories inspired by theories of human cognition. They do not mean that an LLM has the same mental processes as a human.

### Why combine them?

System 1 provides efficient generation, while System 2 introduces additional computation to improve the result.

The goal is not to make every simple question unnecessarily expensive. It is to use more deliberate reasoning when the task requires it.

## 3. A brief history of language models

The lecture places modern self-improving LLMs within the broader development of machine learning.

### Early language modeling

Language modeling focuses on predicting words or tokens based on context.

Earlier approaches used statistical techniques to model language. Neural language models later introduced learned representations of words and more flexible prediction mechanisms.

### Neural networks and representation learning

Neural networks can learn useful representations directly from data and combine them through multiple layers.

Unlike many traditional feature-engineering approaches, neural models can learn internal representations that support different language tasks.

### Attention and Transformers

The development of attention mechanisms and the Transformer architecture made it possible to process relationships between tokens more effectively.

BERT and GPT-style models helped demonstrate the potential of large-scale pretraining.

### The scaling hypothesis

The scaling hypothesis suggests that increasing model size, training data, and computation can improve model capabilities, provided these resources are used effectively.

This contributed to the development of increasingly capable LLMs.

However, larger models can still produce incorrect answers, follow misleading context, or make poor judgments. Scaling alone does not remove the need for reasoning, verification, and alignment.

# Part 2: Why LLMs Make Reasoning Mistakes

## 4. Spurious correlations and irrelevant context

An LLM learns statistical patterns from its training data. Some patterns are useful, but others are accidental or misleading.

A **spurious correlation** is a relationship that appears in data but is not a reliable basis for the conclusion being drawn.

Imagine a question asking for a person's birthplace. The prompt contains several unrelated facts about a city associated with that person.

A model may rely on the frequently mentioned city rather than the actual birthplace.

The answer may sound confident while being incorrect.

### Why does this happen?

An LLM's output depends on its input context. Irrelevant information can influence the generated answer, even when that information should not matter.

The lecture discusses the hypothesis that standard soft attention can distribute attention across many pieces of context, while the language-model training objective can encourage the model to exploit statistical correlations.

This helps explain why a model may be distracted by irrelevant details.

### The solution: Make relevance explicit

A useful approach is to identify the information relevant to the question before generating the final answer.

Instead of treating every piece of context as equally important, the system can first determine what it should use.

This leads to the idea of **System 2 Attention**.

---

## 5. System 2 Attention (S2A)

System 2 Attention is a method for helping an LLM focus on relevant information before answering.

The central idea is simple:

**First rewrite the input to remove irrelevant or biasing information. Then answer the rewritten question.**

### How S2A works

1. Receive the original question and its context.
2. Ask an LLM to identify the actual question and rewrite it without irrelevant information.
3. Give the rewritten input to an LLM to generate the answer.
4. Return the answer based on the more relevant context.

### Example

Suppose the original prompt contains a factual question mixed with unrelated opinions and distracting details.

A normal model might be influenced by those details.

An S2A-style process first rewrites the prompt to retain the factual question and relevant evidence. The answering model then responds to that cleaner input.

### Why is this useful?

It can help reduce:
- Influence from irrelevant context.
- Bias introduced by distracting statements.
- Dependence on misleading associations.

### Limitation

The rewriting step can itself remove useful information or retain misleading details. S2A therefore reduces one source of error; it does not guarantee factual correctness.

---

## 6. Chain-of-Verification (CoVe)

Another technique discussed in the lecture is **Chain-of-Verification (CoVe)**.

The goal is to reduce hallucinations by separating the initial answer from the process of checking its factual claims.

### Basic workflow

1. Generate an initial answer.
2. Create verification questions based on the answer.
3. Answer the verification questions independently.
4. Use the verification results to revise the original answer.

For example, an LLM answering a question about a historical event might identify individual factual claims, check them separately, and revise claims that are unsupported or contradicted.

### Variants of CoVe

The lecture presents three variants.

**1. Joint generation**

The model generates the verification questions and answers in a single left-to-right process.

**2. Factored verification**

The verification answers are generated with access to the verification questions, but are separated from the original answer in the process.

This is intended to reduce the tendency to repeat the original answer's assumptions.

**3. Factor-and-revise**

The process adds an explicit cross-check stage in which the model compares the verification answers with the original claims and revises the answer when they disagree.

### Main lesson

Verification can be more effective when it is separated from the original generation rather than simply asking, "Are you sure?"

However, if the model cannot reliably verify a claim from its own knowledge, external evidence or tools may still be needed.

---

## 7. Sycophancy: When models agree too much

Sycophancy occurs when an LLM agrees with a user's opinion or apparent preference even when that opinion is incorrect.

For example:

User: "I believe this incorrect mathematical result is correct. Can you confirm?"

A sycophantic model may agree rather than independently evaluate the calculation.

This is a problem because helpfulness should not mean automatically agreeing with the user.

A reliable assistant should distinguish between:
- Understanding the user's viewpoint.
- Respectfully responding to the user.
- Determining whether the user's factual claim is correct.

The lecture connects this problem to the broader limitations of models that rely too heavily on learned associations and contextual cues.

More deliberate reasoning, verification, and better training signals can help address these failures.

# Part 3: Breaking Difficult Problems into Smaller Parts

## 8. Branch-Solve-Merge

Complex instructions can be difficult for an LLM to handle as one large task.

**Branch-Solve-Merge** is an approach that decomposes a difficult problem into smaller subproblems, solves them separately, and combines the partial results.

### The process

1. **Branch:** Create a plan that divides the task into subproblems.
2. **Solve:** Generate a solution for each subproblem.
3. **Merge:** Combine the partial solutions into a coherent final response.

### Example: Writing a detailed report

Suppose a task asks an LLM to write a report comparing two technologies.

The system can divide the task into:
- Explain the first technology.
- Explain the second technology.
- Compare their performance.
- Compare their limitations.
- Combine the findings into a final report.

Each branch can be handled separately before the results are merged.

### Why is this useful?

Breaking a task into smaller parts can make it easier to handle long instructions and complex reasoning.

It can also make the system more modular: different subproblems can be solved and evaluated independently.

The lecture presents this method as a way to improve the evaluation and generation of complex language outputs.

### Limitation

The final answer depends on the quality of the decomposition and merging stages. Missing a necessary subproblem or combining inconsistent partial answers can still produce a poor result.

# Part 4: From Better Reasoning to Self-Improving Models

## 9. What is self-training?

Self-training is a family of methods in which a model uses generated or selected data to help train a model further.

A self-improving LLM aims to generate useful examples, evaluate them, and use the selected examples to improve its future performance.

The general loop is:

1. Generate examples or candidate responses.
2. Evaluate their quality.
3. Select useful training examples.
4. Train or fine-tune the model on the selected data.
5. Repeat the process.

This is different from simply asking a model to reconsider an answer during inference.

**Inference-time improvement** changes the process used to produce an answer. **Training-time improvement** updates the model's parameters using training data and an optimization procedure.

The lecture focuses on how the second process can be made more self-directed.

---

## 10. Why is human supervision a bottleneck?

Many modern LLMs are trained using human feedback.

A simplified process is:

1. Humans provide examples of good responses.
2. Models generate alternative responses.
3. Humans compare or rank the responses.
4. Training uses those preferences to improve model behavior.

This approach is useful, but it has a scaling problem.

As LLMs become more capable, their answers can become longer and more specialized. Evaluating every answer may require substantial time and expertise.

For example, a human evaluator may find it difficult to judge a complex scientific explanation without understanding the relevant research field.

The challenge becomes even more significant if we want to supervise AI systems that may eventually outperform human evaluators on particular tasks.

This motivates research into methods that use AI to help generate and evaluate training data.

---

## 11. The weak-to-strong supervision challenge

The **weak-to-strong generalization** problem asks how a less capable supervisor can effectively train a more capable model.

The difficulty is straightforward:

If the student model is more capable than the supervisor, the supervisor may not reliably identify every error or recognize the best answer.

The lecture connects this issue to the broader superalignment challenge: how can humans maintain meaningful oversight as AI systems become more capable?

One possible direction is to use stronger models to assist with evaluation, verification, and supervision.

Another is to develop models that can generate useful training signals themselves.

However, AI-generated supervision must still be evaluated carefully. A model's judgment is not automatically correct just because it is highly capable.

# Part 5: Self-Rewarding Language Models

## 12. What is a self-rewarding LLM?

A **self-rewarding language model** is trained to perform two related tasks:

1. Follow user instructions and generate appropriate responses.
2. Evaluate the quality of responses to instructions.

The same model can then generate candidate answers, score them, and use the scores to construct training examples.

The goal is to improve both its response-generation ability and its ability to evaluate responses.

### Two capabilities

**Instruction following**

Given a user instruction, the model generates a useful answer.

**Evaluation**

Given an instruction, a response, and a scoring rubric, the model estimates how good that response is.

For example, an evaluator might consider whether an answer is relevant, complete, clear, useful, and appropriate for the intended audience.

The model's evaluation can then help determine which examples should be used for further training.

---

## 13. What is a reward model?

A reward model assigns a score or preference signal to an output.

In the context of LLM alignment, the score is intended to represent the quality of a response according to a specified objective.

For example, an evaluator might score a response from 0 to 5.

- 0: Completely unsuitable.
- 3: Partially useful but missing important details.
- 5: Highly relevant, complete, and clear.

These labels are illustrative; actual scoring rubrics need to define the criteria for the task.

A reward model helps compare candidate responses, but its scores are only as reliable as its evaluation capability and training.

---

## 14. How does self-rewarding training work?

The lecture describes a method in which the model improves through repeated data generation and preference training.

### Step 1: Start with a base model

The experiments begin with a pretrained Llama 2 70B model.

The model is initially trained using two types of seed data.

**Instruction-following data (IFT)**

- Input: A user instruction.
- Output: An appropriate response.

The lecture uses instruction-following examples from OpenAssistant, taking the first conversational turn for this data.

**Evaluation data (EFT)**

- Input: An instruction, a response, and a scoring rubric.
- Output: An evaluation with reasoning and a final score.

The model is trained on both instruction-following and evaluation tasks, producing the initial model for the iterative process.

### Step 2: Generate new instructions

The model receives a pool of prompts representing user requirements.

In the experiments, a self-instruction technique is used to create additional prompts from OpenAssistant data.

Ideally, a deployed system could obtain useful prompts from real-world interactions, subject to appropriate privacy and data-handling safeguards.

### Step 3: Generate candidate responses

For each new prompt, the current model generates multiple candidate responses.

The experiments use four candidate responses per prompt.

### Step 4: Score the responses

The model evaluates its own candidate responses using an LLM-as-a-Judge prompt.

The judge uses five additive criteria:

1. **Relevance:** Does the answer address the question?
2. **Coverage:** Does it cover the important requirements?
3. **Usefulness:** Does it help the user accomplish the task?
4. **Clarity:** Is it understandable?
5. **Expertise:** Does it demonstrate appropriate knowledge and competence?

The lecture reports that this rubric-based evaluation performs better than the multiple-choice evaluation format considered in the experiments.

### Step 5: Select preference pairs

The highest-scoring response becomes the preferred response, while the lowest-scoring response becomes the less-preferred response.

The pair is then used as a training example.

For the initial evaluation data, the researchers also use human rankings from OpenAssistant to filter generated judgments. When the model's scores perfectly match the human ranking of four responses, the examples are retained; otherwise, the set is discarded.

This helps provide a more reliable starting point for training the evaluation capability.

### Step 6: Train using DPO

The model is fine-tuned using **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** on the selected preference pairs.

The resulting model is used to generate new candidate responses and judgments in the next iteration.

The experiments perform two self-rewarding training loops, producing subsequent models.

### Step 7: Repeat

The overall process is:

Generate prompts → Generate responses → Score responses → Select preference pairs → Train → Repeat.

The goal is to improve both instruction following and evaluation ability across successive iterations.

---

## 15. What is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)?

DPO is a method for training a language model using preferences between responses.

Instead of requiring a separate reinforcement-learning optimization loop with a learned reward model, DPO directly optimizes the policy using preference data and a reference policy.

A preference example contains:
- A prompt.
- A preferred response.
- A less-preferred response.

The training objective encourages the model to prefer the chosen response over the rejected response in the context of the reference policy.

### Simple example

Prompt:

"Explain machine learning to a beginner."

Response A is clear, accurate, and easy to understand.

Response B is confusing and does not answer the question properly.

A preference dataset records that A is preferred over B.

DPO uses such comparisons to adjust the model's behavior.

In self-rewarding training, the model's own evaluation process helps generate the preference pairs used for DPO.

**Important:** DPO does not independently determine which response is correct. The preference labels must be meaningful. If the model consistently selects poor responses as winners, training can reinforce the wrong behavior.

---

## 16. How did the researchers evaluate self-rewarding models?

The lecture evaluates the models along two main dimensions.

### A. Instruction-following ability

The models are tested on:
- An internal instruction-following set containing 256 prompts from diverse sources.
- AlpacaEval 2.0.
- MT-Bench.

These evaluations assess how well the models respond to user instructions across different tasks.

### B. Reward-model ability

The models are also evaluated on their ability to judge response quality.

This is important because a self-rewarding model must not only produce good responses but also distinguish better responses from worse ones.

The lecture reports that iterative self-rewarding training improves instruction-following performance and evaluation ability in the experiments.

These results demonstrate that self-rewarding training can work under the experimental conditions. They do not prove that every model can improve indefinitely or that self-generated rewards are always reliable.

# Part 6: Teaching Models to Think for General Instructions

## 17. Thinking LLMs: General Instruction Following with Thought Generation

A common assumption is that explicit reasoning is mainly useful for mathematics and programming.

The lecture discusses research extending reasoning-oriented training to general instruction following.

The goal is to train a model to produce thoughts or intermediate reasoning before answering a broader range of user requests.

Examples include:
- Explaining a concept.
- Following multiple writing requirements.
- Comparing alternatives.
- Producing a structured report.
- Answering a question that requires several steps.

### Thought Preference Optimization (TPO)

The lecture introduces **Thought Preference Optimization (TPO)**.

The core idea is to optimize not only the final response, but also the thought-generation process associated with producing the response.

The model generates thoughts and a response. The resulting outputs are evaluated, and preference information is used to improve the model's behavior.

The objective is to make deliberate reasoning useful for general instruction following rather than limiting it to narrowly defined reasoning benchmarks.

### Why is this important?

A user instruction may involve several requirements even when it is not a mathematical problem.

For example:

"Explain neural networks to a beginner, use an analogy, include a small example, and keep the explanation concise."

The model must understand the different requirements and organize a suitable answer.

The lecture reports gains on instruction-following evaluations, including AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard, in the research discussed.

### A key observation

Simply adding a basic chain-of-thought instruction does not automatically produce strong performance.

The lecture emphasizes that the thought-generation process itself may need repeated optimization.

In other words, it is not enough to tell a model to think. The model must learn how to generate useful thoughts for the task.

# Part 7: Meta-Rewarding Language Models

## 18. What is meta-rewarding?

Self-rewarding models focus on improving the responses they generate by scoring their own outputs.

But a new problem appears:

**What if the model's judgments are themselves incorrect?**

For example, a model might consistently prefer long, confident answers over shorter, more accurate ones.

Even if the model generates many preference pairs, poor evaluation can prevent meaningful improvement.

Meta-rewarding addresses this issue by adding another level of evaluation.

### The three roles

A meta-rewarding model combines three roles.

**1. Actor**

Generates responses to user instructions.

**2. Judge**

Evaluates the quality of the generated responses.

**3. Meta-judge**

Evaluates the quality of the judge's judgments.

The meta-judge adds a new signal that can help the system improve its evaluation capability rather than focusing only on response generation.

---

## 19. How does meta-rewarding work?

The lecture describes a three-stage iterative process.

### Step 1: Create actor data

The model generates candidate responses and evaluates them using its own judging capability.

This produces responses and self-rewards.

### Step 2: Create judge data

A meta-judge evaluates the quality of the judgments made by the model.

The goal is to identify which judgments are more reliable.

The lecture describes generating multiple judgments for pairs of responses, constructing pairwise meta-judgments, and calculating Elo scores from the resulting comparisons.

**Elo scores** are numerical ratings used to compare competitors based on pairwise outcomes. Here, they are adapted to compare the quality of judgments.

The resulting rankings help create preference pairs for training the judge.

### Step 3: Train using DPO

DPO is used on preference data to improve the model's response-generation and judgment capabilities.

The process is then repeated.

The overall loop is:

Generate responses → Judge responses → Evaluate judgments → Train on preferences → Repeat.

### Why add the meta-judge?

Without this extra stage, the system may improve its ability to generate answers while leaving its evaluation ability weak.

Meta-rewarding provides additional training information about the quality of the judgments themselves.

---

## 20. Controlling response length

The lecture also discusses length control during preference optimization.

Longer answers are not automatically better. They may contain useful detail, but they can also include unnecessary repetition.

The method described uses a length-control approach when selecting preferred responses.

When two responses receive similar scores and both are good, the shorter response can be selected as the preferred one.

This encourages concise answers when extra length does not provide additional value.

The lecture reports that the meta-rewarding method outperforms self-rewarding under the comparison using the same length-control method.

It also reports higher agreement with a GPT-4 judge, suggesting that better judgments can help explain the improvement in response quality.

### Important lesson

A good evaluator should reward the qualities that matter for the task rather than automatically rewarding length, confidence, or stylistic polish.

# Part 8: EvalPlanner — Teaching LLMs to Reason About Evaluations

## 21. Why does evaluation need reasoning?

Evaluating a response is not always easy.

Consider two answers to a technical question. One might be clearer, while the other includes more complete information. Determining which is better requires understanding the user's requirements and weighing different aspects of quality.

A simple score may not reveal how the evaluator reached its decision.

The lecture presents **EvalPlanner**, a method for training a model to reason more effectively when evaluating responses.

The aim is to develop a thinking LLM-as-a-Judge: a model that uses planning and reasoning to make better judgments.

## 22. Turning evaluation into a verifiable task

Directly evaluating the quality of a response can be difficult because there may be no single objectively correct answer.

The lecture describes constructing synthetic preference data to make evaluation more verifiable.

### The basic idea

1. Generate a prompt \(x\).
2. Generate a good response \(y\) for that prompt.
3. Create a similar but different prompt \(x'\).
4. Generate a good response \(y'\) for the new prompt.
5. Ask the evaluator to judge the responses in relation to the original task.
6. Use the known task relationship to create a training signal.

The key is that the two responses are constructed so that the correct preference is known from how they relate to the task.

For example, a response designed for one question should not automatically be preferred when it is being evaluated against a different question.

The model can learn to recognize whether a response actually satisfies the intended instruction.

### Why create similar but different prompts?

The prompts must be related enough to generate comparable responses, but different enough to make relevance and task alignment meaningful.

The lecture notes that an LLM can itself be asked to create these related prompts.

This helps produce synthetic evaluation data without requiring a human to write every example.

---

## 23. Planning and reasoning in EvalPlanner

EvalPlanner trains the evaluator to generate a plan or reasoning process before making a judgment.

The lecture reports two important observations:

- Plans perform better than having no thoughts.
- During training, plans should be relatively unconstrained rather than forced into fixed formats such as lists of evaluation criteria or verification questions.

The model should learn how to reason about the evaluation task instead of merely following a rigid checklist.

The approach trains a model to evaluate response quality using generated reasoning and synthetic preference data.

The lecture reports strong results on RewardBench compared with other LLM-as-a-Judge models, using a Llama 3.1 70B base model, as well as strong performance on more difficult evaluation benchmarks discussed in the talk.

These results suggest that training the evaluator's reasoning ability can improve judgment quality.

# Part 9: How the Approaches Fit Together

The lecture presents a progression from improving individual answers to improving the process that produces and evaluates answers.

| Approach | Main goal | How it helps |
|---|---|---|
| System 2 Attention | Focus on relevant information | Rewrites input to reduce distracting context |
| Chain-of-Verification | Reduce factual errors | Generates and checks verification questions |
| Branch-Solve-Merge | Handle complex tasks | Solves subproblems and combines the results |
| Self-Rewarding | Improve responses and evaluation | Generates responses, scores them, and trains on preferences |
| Thinking LLMs / TPO | Improve general instruction following | Optimizes the model's thought generation and responses |
| Meta-Rewarding | Improve the quality of judgments | Uses a meta-judge to evaluate the model's evaluations |
| EvalPlanner | Improve reasoning during evaluation | Trains evaluators to plan and reason about response quality |

These methods target different weaknesses. They are not interchangeable, and combining them requires careful evaluation.

# Part 10: Limitations and Future Directions

## 24. Why self-improvement can fail

A self-improving system depends on the quality of its training signals.

If the model generates poor examples, scores them incorrectly, or selects the wrong responses, the next training cycle may reinforce those mistakes.

This creates several risks:

- Incorrect judgments become training labels.
- The model learns to optimize its own flawed scoring rules.
- Repeated training amplifies weaknesses instead of correcting them.
- The system becomes better at producing responses that score well without actually satisfying the user's needs.

Therefore, self-improvement requires reliable evaluation, suitable data filtering, and independent tests.

## 25. Why better evaluation is a major bottleneck

A central message of the lecture is that self-evaluation can limit the performance of a self-improving system.

If the model cannot distinguish a strong response from a weak one, it cannot reliably construct useful preference data.

This motivates spending additional computation on evaluation, using better judgment models, and training evaluators to reason more carefully.

The lecture also suggests that interaction with people, the external world, the internet, and the model's own experience may provide further opportunities for learning.

These directions connect self-improvement with AI agents, which can take actions, observe results, and learn from interaction.

## 26. Learning from interaction

An AI agent can receive information from:
- Human feedback.
- Tool outputs.
- Environment responses.
- Search results.
- Its own previous attempts.

Such interaction can generate useful training data, especially when outcomes can be checked.

For example, a coding agent can execute a program and observe whether it passes tests. A task-solving agent can receive feedback from its environment.

The important distinction is between generating a plausible explanation and obtaining evidence that a result is correct.

## 27. Latent reasoning: COCONUT

The lecture briefly introduces a different direction for reasoning: **COCONUT**, a method that explores reasoning in a continuous latent space rather than expressing every intermediate reasoning step as ordinary text tokens.

In conventional chain-of-thought reasoning, intermediate thoughts are represented as text.

Latent reasoning investigates whether some reasoning can instead occur through internal representations that are not directly expressed as natural-language tokens.

This is a research direction rather than proof that textual reasoning is unnecessary or that latent reasoning is always better.

It raises an interesting question about the future of LLMs: can models reason more effectively using internal representations while still producing understandable final answers?

# 28. Final Summary

The lecture explores how LLMs can become more capable by improving both their reasoning and their ability to evaluate their own outputs.

The main lessons are:

1. **System 1 and System 2:** Fast generation is useful, but complex problems may require additional planning, search, and verification.
2. **Reduce irrelevant context:** System 2 Attention attempts to focus the model on the information needed to answer a question.
3. **Verify claims:** Chain-of-Verification separates initial generation from the process of checking factual claims.
4. **Decompose complex tasks:** Branch-Solve-Merge divides difficult problems into smaller parts and combines their solutions.
5. **Self-rewarding models:** A model can generate candidate responses, score them, and use preference optimization to improve future responses.
6. **Thought optimization:** Thinking LLMs and TPO explore how reasoning can improve general instruction following.
7. **Meta-rewarding:** A meta-judge evaluates the quality of the model's judgments, helping improve the evaluator itself.
8. **EvalPlanner:** Planning and reasoning can help a model evaluate response quality more effectively.
9. **Future research:** Better evaluation, interaction-based learning, and latent reasoning may support further improvements.
